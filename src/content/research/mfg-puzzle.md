## The Right Question to Ask

People often point to one number. India's manufacturing sector has been stuck between 13 and 17 percent of GDP for many decades. China sits at 26 percent. Vietnam is at 24.5 percent. The conclusion seems obvious — "India failed at manufacturing."

But that framing has a flaw in it. When the services sector grows very quickly, manufacturing's share of the economy falls even when manufacturing itself is expanding. India's total manufacturing output reached **$461 billion in 2023** and continues to grow. India also officially released its new GDP series with base year 2022–23 in February 2026, and analysis of this new data shows manufacturing's share rising from 14.5 to 16.3 percent in the new series. The widely repeated 14 percent figure may partly reflect a measurement gap and not just a structural one.

The more useful questions are these: Why hasn't manufacturing created enough jobs? And why can't India compete more effectively in global export markets?

## Why India Chose Services — And Why That Is No Longer Enough

After 1991, India's move into IT and services was not accidental. It followed the logic of what the country actually had. Millions of educated, English-speaking workers — yes. Reliable roads, steady electricity, and accessible land — not really. So investment, talent, and government attention all flowed toward services. By FY2024–25, India's IT exports reached **$224 billion**, growing 12.48 percent year-on-year, contributing nearly 8 percent of GDP.

<div class="data-note"><strong>The arithmetic problem:</strong> IT services employ roughly 5.8 million professionals in total. India adds approximately 12 million new workers to its labour force every year. High-value services employment cannot absorb that volume at scale.</div>

Manufacturing, even at moderate productivity levels, generates far denser employment per unit of investment and can provide work for people without college degrees — which describes most of India's annual labour force entrants. The services bet was well-reasoned in the 1990s. By the 2010s, it was no longer sufficient on its own.

## What Held Manufacturing Back — and What Is Changing

**Moving goods cost too much.** The commonly cited figure of 13 to 14 percent of GDP came from partial datasets. The first comprehensive DPIIT-NCAER assessment, published in September 2025, puts logistics costs at **7.97 percent of GDP** for FY2023–24 — a significant drop. The Economic Survey 2025–26 attributes this to PM Gati Shakti, dedicated freight corridors, and the Unified Logistics Interface Platform.

**Labour laws created a size trap, but infrastructure explains more.** India had over 40 central labour laws, including provisions requiring government approval before a firm with more than 100 employees could reduce its workforce. However, researchers at NIPFP have found that infrastructure quality and credit access explain more of India's manufacturing gap than labour regulation does.

**Land acquisition proved politically impossible to resolve at scale.** Building a large factory required navigating fragmented land records, multiple state-level clearances, and the politically sensitive process of relocating communities. The answer has to be industrial zones where land is pre-cleared before investors arrive — not expecting each firm to fight through the process alone.

## What the China and Vietnam Comparison Actually Teaches Us

China's manufacturing-first model lifted 800 million people out of poverty, but by 2025–26 it is showing serious strain. Overcapacity in steel, electric vehicles, and solar panels has led to prices falling because Chinese consumers aren't buying enough of what factories produce. More importantly, China's path depended on a highly centralised state and suppressed wages that India's democratic process would not permit.

Vietnam, meanwhile, signed trade agreements aggressively and integrated quickly into global supply chains. It worked — but Samsung alone accounts for nearly one-fifth of Vietnam's exports, and growth slowed to 6.7 percent in 2025 as global demand softened. Optimising entirely for integration speed leaves an economy exposed to those specific chains.

> The lesson is not that India should have copied either model. It is that sustained manufacturing growth requires action on infrastructure, trade access, land, and labour simultaneously — not in sequence.

## Reforms: What Worked, What Didn't, and the Gaps That Remain

The **Four Labour Codes** consolidate over 40 central laws into cleaner frameworks and most major industrial states have begun implementing them by early 2026. But while factory regulation is being simplified, several states have moved in the opposite direction for gig workers. Telangana passed India's first Gig Workers' Bill in 2024, with Karnataka and Jharkhand following in 2025–26. The result is an emerging two-tier system: formal manufacturing getting simpler, while the delivery and logistics layer grows more regulated.

**GST is probably the clearest policy success of the past decade.** Removing inter-state checkpoints and reducing compliance burdens has compounded quietly into real logistics improvement over time.

**PLI schemes have delivered highly uneven results.** Electronics exports crossed $47 billion in 2025, up 37 percent year-on-year, with Apple iPhone shipments accounting for nearly two-thirds of the total. But leather exports fell nearly 10 percent in FY24 with continued pressure in FY25. PLI works well when it attracts industries built around one large global company. It works far less well for traditional, labour-intensive industries with thin margins.

## The Informal Economy: The Problem Behind the Problem

Around 80 to 81 percent of India's workforce is employed informally, according to the ILO. This is sometimes described as providing economic flexibility. That framing is misleading. Informality at this scale reflects the fact that the formal economy cannot profitably employ most workers under current conditions.

Informal firms are significantly less productive than formal ones. They cannot access institutional credit at reasonable rates, invest less in technology, and offer workers no job security or social protection. Moving workers into better-paying formal employment requires not just simpler labour law but also accessible credit and vocational training connected to actual industry demand.

## The Hybrid Model: Real Strength or Convenient Story?

A growing argument holds that India's combination of services and manufacturing is a strategic advantage — more stable than China's industrial concentration and more sustainable than Vietnam's export dependence. The real advantages exist. India's IT sector generates high-productivity employment and earns hard currency. Digital public infrastructure like UPI, Aadhaar, and the account aggregator framework has reduced transaction costs across the economy. India's large domestic market means the economy isn't entirely dependent on what other countries decide to buy.

But the risk is using this framing to make the current situation sound intentional. A hybrid model is only a genuine strength when both parts are working well. If manufacturing remains weak and fails to absorb the labour force at a meaningful scale, then describing that outcome as a strategic hybrid is just foolish.

## The Honest Bottom Line

India's manufacturing story is not one of failure. It is the story of a country that made a rational bet in 1991, rode it successfully for three decades, and now needs to build a second engine before the demographic window narrows. India's working-age population ratio will peak in roughly 10 to 15 years. After that point, the advantage that makes labour-intensive manufacturing attractive begins to diminish.

The direction in 2026 is encouraging: logistics costs confirmed below 8 percent, labour codes being implemented, electronics exports nearly doubled. But the unevenness is real. High-tech PLI sectors are rising fast while traditional labour-intensive exports contract. Export orders are softening just as domestic sentiment peaks. Gig economy regulation is adding complexity at precisely the moment factory regulation is being simplified.

<div class="conclusion">

The hybrid model can be a genuine strength. But only if manufacturing actually earns its place in it — not just gets described as part of it.

</div>
