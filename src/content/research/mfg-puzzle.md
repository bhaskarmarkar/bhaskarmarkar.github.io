## The Right Question to Ask

India's manufacturing sector has been stuck between 13 and 17 percent of GDP for decades, against 26 percent in China and 24.5 percent in Vietnam. That comparison gets used to declare India a manufacturing failure fairly often.

It's a share-of-GDP problem more than an output problem. When services grow fast, manufacturing's slice of the pie shrinks even while the pie itself grows. India's manufacturing output hit **$461 billion in 2023**, still climbing. The new GDP series, base year 2022-23, released in February 2026, actually revises manufacturing's share upward from 14.5 to 16.3 percent once the data is recalculated. So the 14 percent figure everyone quotes is partly a measurement artifact, not just a structural fact.

Why manufacturing hasn't created enough jobs, and why India still can't compete in export markets the way it should: those are the questions worth asking instead.

## Why India Chose Services, and Why That Is No Longer Enough

After 1991, the move into IT and services wasn't an accident. It followed what the country actually had to work with. Plenty of educated, English-speaking workers. Not much in the way of reliable roads, steady power, or land you could actually build on. So capital, talent, and policy attention all went where the comparative advantage was. By FY2024-25, IT exports had reached **$224 billion**, up 12.48 percent year-on-year, and now contribute nearly 8 percent of GDP.

<div class="data-note"><strong>The arithmetic problem:</strong> IT services employ roughly 5.8 million professionals in total. India adds approximately 12 million new workers to its labour force every year. High-value services employment cannot absorb that volume at scale.</div>

Manufacturing, even at moderate productivity levels, generates far denser employment per unit of investment and can provide work for people without college degrees. That describes most of India's annual labour force entrants. The services bet was well-reasoned in the 1990s. By the 2010s, it was no longer sufficient on its own.

## What Held Manufacturing Back, and What Is Changing

Logistics costs are the clearest case of a number that was simply wrong. The 13-14 percent of GDP figure everyone cited for years came from partial datasets. The first comprehensive DPIIT-NCAER assessment, published in September 2025, puts the real figure at **7.97 percent of GDP** for FY2023-24. The Economic Survey 2025-26 credits PM Gati Shakti, the dedicated freight corridors, and the Unified Logistics Interface Platform.

Labour law gets blamed disproportionately. India had over 40 central labour laws on the books, including rules requiring government sign-off before any firm above 100 employees could downsize. Real, but NIPFP researchers who've actually run the numbers find infrastructure quality and credit access explain more of the manufacturing gap than labour regulation does.

Land is the one nobody's really solved. Building a large factory meant wading through fragmented land records, multiple state-level clearances, and the politically radioactive process of relocating communities. Industrial zones with land pre-cleared before investors show up are the only approach that's worked. Leaving each firm to fight the process alone doesn't scale.

## What the China and Vietnam Comparison Actually Teaches Us

China's manufacturing-first model pulled 800 million people out of poverty. By 2025-26 it's straining under its own success: overcapacity in steel, EVs, and solar has pushed prices down because domestic consumers aren't absorbing what the factories churn out. And the model depended on a centralised state and suppressed wages, conditions India's democracy was never going to replicate.

Vietnam went the other way, signing trade agreements aggressively and plugging fast into global supply chains. It worked, mostly, though Samsung alone accounts for nearly a fifth of Vietnam's exports, and growth slowed to 6.7 percent in 2025 as global demand cooled. Optimise for integration speed and you end up exposed to whichever chains you optimised for.

> Neither model is a template to copy. What both show is that infrastructure, trade access, land, and labour have to move together, not one at a time.

## Reforms: What Worked, What Didn't, and the Gaps That Remain

The Four Labour Codes consolidate 40-plus central laws into cleaner frameworks, and most major industrial states have started implementing them by early 2026. Meanwhile, several states have gone the opposite direction for gig workers: Telangana passed India's first Gig Workers' Bill in 2024, and Karnataka and Jharkhand followed in 2025-26. So factory regulation gets simpler right as the delivery and logistics layer gets more regulated. A two-tier system forming almost by accident.

GST is probably the clearest policy win of the decade. Killing inter-state checkpoints and cutting compliance overhead has quietly compounded into real logistics gains over ten years.

PLI is a mixed bag, and not a small one. Electronics exports crossed $47 billion in 2025, up 37 percent year-on-year, though nearly two-thirds of that is Apple iPhone shipments. Leather exports, meanwhile, fell almost 10 percent in FY24 with the pressure continuing into FY25. The scheme works when it can attach itself to one large global company's supply chain. For thin-margin, labour-intensive industries without that anchor, it hasn't done much.

## The Informal Economy: The Problem Behind the Problem

Roughly 80 to 81 percent of India's workforce works informally, per ILO figures. People like to call this flexibility. It isn't. At that scale, informality just means the formal economy can't profitably employ most workers under current conditions.

Informal firms run less productive than formal ones almost by definition: no real access to institutional credit, little investment in technology, and no job security or social protection for workers. Getting people into better formal jobs takes more than simpler labour law. It needs credit that's actually accessible and vocational training tied to what industries actually need, not what looks good in a policy document.

## The Hybrid Model: Real Strength or Convenient Story?

There's a growing argument that India's mix of services and manufacturing is itself the strategy: steadier than China's industrial concentration, less exposed than Vietnam's export dependence. Some of that holds up. IT generates high-productivity jobs and hard currency. UPI, Aadhaar, and the account aggregator framework have cut transaction costs economy-wide. A domestic market this large means growth isn't hostage to what other countries feel like buying this quarter.

Where it falls apart is when the framing gets used to make the current mess sound deliberate. A hybrid only counts as a strength if both halves are actually pulling weight — and manufacturing still isn't absorbing labour at the scale that matters.

## Where This Leaves Things

None of this reads as failure so much as a country that made a rational bet in 1991, rode it for three decades, and now has to build a second engine before its demographic window closes. The working-age population ratio peaks in roughly 10 to 15 years; after that, the advantage that makes labour-intensive manufacturing attractive starts fading.

2026's direction is genuinely encouraging: logistics costs confirmed under 8 percent, labour codes rolling out, electronics exports nearly doubled. But it's lopsided. PLI-favoured sectors are racing ahead while traditional labour-intensive exports shrink, export orders are softening right as domestic sentiment peaks, and gig regulation is piling on complexity at the exact moment factory rules are getting simpler.

The hybrid model can work. It just has to actually work, not just get described that way.
