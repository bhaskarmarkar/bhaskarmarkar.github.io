### An 800 km Dash to Peace

I didn't ease into Japan — I launched into it.

Within minutes of landing, I was aboard the Nozomi Shinkansen, hurtling through the green fields of Honshu at 285 km/h. Tokyo faded into abstraction as the landscape blurred past, and by mid-afternoon, I had already crossed half the island to reach Hiroshima — a global symbol of peace and resilience.

Walking into the Peace Memorial Park at sunset felt like stepping into a different world. The A-Bomb Dome, its skeletal steel frame reflected in the Motoyasu River, stood in a silence that felt heavy yet necessary. After a morning defined by speed, this place demanded stillness.

Ending the day on such a sombre note felt right. Yet Hiroshima doesn't just carry its past — it carries a quiet resilience. Wandering through its alleyways, I found my way to Okonomimura in search of okonomiyaki, a dish I had first tasted nearly twenty years ago. Warm, comforting, and deeply familiar, it grounded me after a day that had moved fast but landed somewhere meaningful.

### Shadows of the Past, Spirits of the Sea

If Day 1 was about resilience, Day 2 was about understanding it.

Inside the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum, the silence was intense. Scorched artifacts transformed abstract history into something deeply human, something that lingered long after you moved on.

Stepping outside felt like resurfacing.

By afternoon, I was on a ferry to Miyajima Island. The famous floating torii gate rose from the water in brilliant red, framed by the blue sea. The shift in atmosphere was immediate — the heaviness of the morning softened into something lighter, calmer.

I spent the afternoon wandering along the shoreline, dodging the island's persistent yet oddly charming deer. Between stops, I sampled local specialities — warm momiji manju, rich matcha ice cream, and a perfectly charred serving of anago meshi.

### History, Neon, and Street Food

Leaving Hiroshima behind, the Shinkansen dropped me straight into Osaka's pulse.

I began at Osaka Castle, an imposing fortress surrounded by massive stone walls, standing firm against a modern skyline. But Osaka doesn't reveal itself in its monuments — it reveals itself in its streets.

Dotonbori was a sensory explosion. Neon lights flashed, giant mechanical signs moved overhead, and the air carried the smell of batter, grilled meat, and sugar.

I followed the rhythm of the street — starting with freshly made takoyaki, watching vendors flip them with effortless precision, then moving to a warm melon pan with its crisp, sugary crust, and finally ending with Kobe A5 wagyu that practically melted the moment it touched my tongue.

> Osaka wasn't just something I saw — it was something I felt.

### Kyoto and Nara — Tradition and Scale

After Osaka's neon rush, Kyoto felt like stepping back in time.

The days unfolded through Japan's cultural heart — the golden stillness of Kinkakuji, the endless torii gates of Fushimi Inari stretching into the hills, and the sweeping sunset views from Kiyomizudera. But not everything was quiet. Watching mochi being made — the rhythmic pounding with perfect timing, turning tradition into spectacle.

Hostel life added an unexpected layer. Evenings turned into shared conversations with travellers from around the world, swapping stories, laughing at cultural differences, and bonding over small confusions. What started as a quiet exploration turned into one of the most social parts of the trip, culminating in an impromptu bar crawl that ended the best way possible — getting lost in a new city with new friends.

Nara brought a different perspective. At Todai-ji Temple, the Great Buddha felt almost unreal in its scale. Outside, the famous deer added a playful contrast bowing as people offer them food as a tradition.

### The Shy Peak of Fuji

From Kyoto, I headed toward Kawaguchiko with one goal: to see Mount Fuji.

It never appeared.

Fuji lived up to its reputation — hidden behind a thick layer of clouds. I spent the entire day "Fuji hunting," scanning the horizon and waiting for a break that never came.

And yet, it didn't feel like a failure.

Walking along the lake, surrounded by mist and stillness, I found something unexpectedly calming. Without the mountain dominating the view, everything else became clearer. That night, a warm bowl of tonkotsu ramen at a small hilltop izakaya became the highlight.

> Sometimes, the hunt is the experience.

### Tokyo — The Perfect Finale

Returning to Tokyo felt like stepping into a different reality — a city that never slows down.

#### Day 7 — Nostalgia, sumo, and surrealism

Leaving Kawaguchiko, I was looking forward to one of my main motives — visiting the place where I lived 20 years ago. Heading to the Nakano area, a relatively less modern part of Tokyo, I walked streets that felt both foreign and familiar. Going through Broadway brought back memories that had subconsciously stayed with me. Locating the konbini and barber shop, memories of being sent on grocery runs as a toddler came flooding back.

Meeting with family friends and reliving those memories brought a satisfaction that you can only truly understand by experiencing it yourself.

After a nostalgic morning, the afternoon began with a sumo experience, watching wrestlers up close. The power, precision, and ritual felt far more intense in person than on screen. By night, I was at TeamLab Planets — walking through water and fields of light. It felt surreal, like stepping into a dream.

#### Day 8 — Volcanoes and the shy mountain again

Hakone brought a completely different landscape — steam rising from Owakudani's volcanic vents, the air thick with sulfur. I tried the famous black eggs, said to add seven years to your life. Once again, Fuji stayed hidden, but by now, that felt like part of the story.

By evening I was back in Tokyo, heading to Asakusa as the sun dropped. Senso-ji at night felt entirely different from the daytime crowds I had imagined. Walking through the massive red Kaminarimon gate, the temple glowed softly under lantern light.

#### Day 9 — The grand finale

I didn't plan my last full day — I let Tokyo decide.

It started quietly at Meiji Shrine, where the city disappears behind towering trees. By afternoon, I stepped into the opposite extreme. At Shibuya Crossing, I waited as the signal turned red. Then, all at once, the crowd surged from every direction — hundreds of people moving in perfect chaos. Standing in the middle, everything seemed to pause for a second: light, sound, motion, all colliding.

From there, I drifted into Akihabara — neon, arcades, and endless rows of collectables. Somewhere in that blur, I stepped into Don Quijote — and somehow, things got even louder. I walked in for a quick look and walked out much later with things I never planned to buy.

As night settled in, I moved to Shinjuku. The energy shifted again — tighter streets, glowing signs, and social interactions in the numerous izakayas. Messy, crowded, alive. And the perfect way to end.

### The Food, and the Moments In Between

Food in Japan wasn't just part of the trip — it shaped it.

Some meals were planned. Most weren't. Late nights often ended in ramen shops where everything moved with quiet efficiency — ticket machines, quick service, no wasted motion. In Kyoto, the omurice stood out — not just for its taste, but for the performance. Watching the chef slice open a perfectly cooked omelette and let it spill over the rice felt like precision meeting comfort.

But the moments that stayed weren't always the big ones. They were the in-between: grabbing an egg sando and karaage from a konbini that somehow exceeded expectations, standing in front of a vending machine overwhelmed by choice, eating on a train platform — slightly rushed, slightly tired, but completely content.

> Japan makes even convenience feel intentional.

### What Stayed

In ten days, Japan moved fast — bullet trains, packed streets, constant transitions.

But somewhere between Hiroshima's silence, Kyoto's culture, and a cloud-covered Fuji, the journey slowed me down.

And what stayed with me wasn't just what I saw — it was what I heard. The rhythmic chirping of pedestrian crossings. The quiet of temple grounds, broken only by the rustle of trees. The melodic train announcements echoing through stations, turning daily commutes into something oddly calming. And cutting through it all, the unmistakable looping jingle inside Don Quijote — chaotic, catchy, and impossible to forget.

Beyond the sounds, there was something more satisfying — the small victories of language. The subtle confidence of an "arigato gozaimasu" landing naturally, or starting a conversation with "sumimasen" and, for a second, feeling like you belong and aren't just a gaijin.

Each place had its own rhythm. Each moment, its own soundtrack. It wasn't just about how far I travelled — it was about how differently each place made me feel.
